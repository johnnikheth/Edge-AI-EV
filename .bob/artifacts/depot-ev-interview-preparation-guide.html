3. The Problem (why it matters)

Four problems costing fleet owners money:

  • Peak-price charging: Vans return 13:00–14:30 at 15–20% battery. Charging on arrival runs straight through the PG&E peak (4–9 pm, $0.357/kWh — more than twice off-peak).
  • Demand & capacity limits: 5 × 7.7 kW chargers + 30 kW HVAC = 68 kW on a 50 kW service → 7 breaker trips per night.
  • Wasted rooftop solar: Solar produces in the afternoon but no one plans charging around it — surplus exported for nothing.
  • Delivery disruption: Traffic, accidents and breakdowns cause late deliveries. Customers not notified, stops not rebalanced.

7. Likely Interview Questions & Answers

Q: What problem does Depot EV solve?
Electric delivery vans return in the afternoon and charge right through PG&E's expensive 4–9 pm peak at $0.357/kWh — more than twice the off-peak rate. Without a managed system, that costs the depot about $4,200/month and trips the site breaker 7 times a night. Depot EV cuts the bill by 53% to about $2,000/month by planning all charging into cheaper hours, using rooftop solar and EV storage, and controlling the HVAC to keep the site under its limit.
Q: How does the charging planner work?
It's a least-cost scheduler. For each van — in order of earliest departure — it places the energy the route needs into the cheapest available slots: free solar first, then stored solar at peak (treated as already paid for), then super off-peak grid ($0.138), then off-peak ($0.165). Peak grid ($0.357) is used only if a van would otherwise miss its departure. Top-ups above the route requirement never use peak or storage.
Q: What is the agent hierarchy?
There are 11 agents in three layers. At the bottom: 8 specialist agents each own one decision (Power, Solar & Storage, Tariff, Outage resilience, Demand response, Fleet planner, Dispatch, Battery health, Charger faults, Customer comms, Reporting). Above them: 3 command agents (Energy, Fleet, Business) each supervise their group and reach one GO / WATCH / ACTION NEEDED conclusion. The worst of the three becomes the depot verdict shown on the Agents tab and in the morning brief.
Q: What is the role of IBM Bob vs watsonx Granite vs Orchestrate?
Bob is the development tool — it built, tested, secured and deployed the solution using 80 credits. It is not part of the running app. Granite is the AI inside the running app — it writes the morning brief and customer messages at runtime. Orchestrate is the operations interface — a read-only agent that answers the depot manager's morning questions using saved session data via 5 OpenAPI tools.
Q: What is "human in the loop" in this system?
The agents act autonomously on low-risk decisions (rerouting for traffic, HVAC, load balancing) but always pause for a person on higher-risk ones: reassigning delivery stops between vans, rescheduling customers, and approving customer messages before they're sent. The dispatcher clicks Approve or Reject in the app. Every decision is logged for audit. This is enforced in code — the dispatch agent never sends a message or moves a stop without an approval event in the database.
Q: Why does the system give vans priority over the building for stored energy?
An earlier version let the building drain storage at peak when HVAC was forced on. The vans that were planned to charge from storage then had only 12 kW of grid capacity overnight and three missed their routes. Giving vans first claim on stored energy fixed this in every scenario, at a cost of about $7 a night in normal conditions.
Q: What is the PG&E BEV-1 subscription and why does it matter?
BEV-1 replaces traditional demand charges with a subscription of $12.41 per 10 kW block per month, based on the peak 15-minute demand. The planner minimises peak demand by spreading charging across hours and vans — keeping the subscription to as few 10 kW blocks as possible. This is one of the hidden savings that smart charging delivers beyond just moving energy out of peak hours.
Q: What happens during a grid outage?
Solar and EV storage (including the reserve) automatically power the entire depot — chargers and building — at any hour, peak included. HVAC is shed to extend the runtime. The most urgent vans charge first. When the grid returns, storage recharges from solar plus grid. All 5 vans were still ready in the outage scenario tested.

(3) Deploy on IBM Cloud Code Engine with a permanent HTTPS URL so Orchestrate can reach the API without a tunnel — the Dockerfile and deploy script are already written, we just need Docker and a resource-group permission.
